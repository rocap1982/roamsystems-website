# Smart Storage Solutions for Your Campervan Build

Storage is the part of a campervan build that people underestimate until they're three days into a trip and can't find the kettle. A van is a small space being asked to do a lot of jobs — kitchen, bedroom, wardrobe, gear locker, sometimes office — and the difference between a build that works and one that quietly drives you mad usually comes down to how the storage was planned.

This guide walks through how to think about campervan storage in the UK: where the space lives in a typical conversion, the trade-offs between different drawer systems, and the small details that decide whether a clever idea on paper actually works in practice.

---

## Where Storage Lives in a Campervan

In a SWB van — a VW Transporter T5, T6, T6.1, or Ford Transit Custom — there are really only four zones where meaningful storage can live:

1. **Under the bed / seating** — the largest single storage area in most conversions
2. **At the rear** — accessible through the tailgate without disturbing the interior
3. **Overhead** — lockers above the kitchen or seating
4. **Vertical wall cabinets** — wardrobe-style storage, usually behind the driver's seat

The right answer for any individual build is a combination of these, sized to what you actually carry. A weekend couple needs less than a family of four heading away for a fortnight, and a build that prioritises mountain bikes is laid out differently to one that prioritises a wetsuit and surfboard. The starting point is being honest about what's coming with you.

## Rear Pull-Out Systems: Maximum Capacity

If you're carrying a lot — long camping trips, family kit, expedition gear — a rear pull-out drawer is the single biggest upgrade you can make to a campervan's storage. It lives at the back of the van, slides out through the tailgate, and gives you instant access to a large volume of storage without climbing inside.

The [Rear Pull-Out Drawer Storage System](/products/rear-pull-out-drawer-storage-system) provides over 330 litres of space — meaningfully more than the central under-bed drawer — on the same 220 kg runner system. It's the same construction philosophy: laser-cut and folded aluminium, textured black powder coat, CNC-cut phenolic plywood front panel and lid.

The lid matters more than people expect. When the drawer is closed, the lid becomes a useful work surface — somewhere to make a sandwich, drop a wet jacket, or stage gear before loading it inside. A drawer system without a robust lid loses you that surface entirely.

Plenty of conversions run **both** the central under-bed drawer and the rear pull-out — one for daily items reached from the back of the bed, one for bulkier gear accessed through the tailgate. It's a combination that scales with how much you carry.

## Overhead Lockers: Lightweight Storage Up High

Overhead lockers are for the small, frequently-used items that are awkward to dig out of a drawer — mugs, plates, dry food, head torches, the second tea towel. The trick is to use them for lightweight items only. Anything heavy stored high in a van changes the centre of gravity and gets thrown around in any sudden braking.

Our [Aluminium Overhead Locker](/products/aluminium-overhead-locker) is available in 105 cm (SWB) and 133 cm (LWB) versions, designed to sit above the [Kitchen Pod Campervan Package](/products/kitchen-pod-campervan-package). It uses Blum soft-close hinges, a secure travel latch, and integrated water-resistant LED under-lighting that lights the worktop below.

The travel latch is non-negotiable. A locker door that pops open over rough roads at 60 mph is genuinely dangerous — anything inside becomes a projectile. Cheaper lockers use magnetic catches that fail under vibration; positive mechanical latches don't.

## The Small Details That Make Storage Actually Work

There's a pattern in campervan builds where the storage looks great on paper, then quietly disappoints once you live with it. A few details separate the two:

**Lockable runners.** When you're driving, drawers that aren't positively locked will rattle open over bumps, then slam closed under braking. Heavy-duty lockable runners (rated for the loads campervans actually carry, not domestic furniture loads) solve this completely. All of the ROAM Systems drawer products use 220 kg lockable runners for exactly this reason.

**Loading height.** A drawer at knee height that opens easily through the tailgate is dramatically more usable than a drawer you have to crouch to reach. This matters more on long trips, in poor weather, and as your back gets older.

**Wet/dry separation.** A wet wetsuit, muddy boots, or a dripping cool box dumped onto soft-finish storage damages it permanently. An aluminium drawer with a phenolic plywood lid takes that abuse without complaint. A laminated MDF drawer doesn't.

**Visibility.** You'll spend more time looking for things in a campervan than in a house, because everything moves around. Drawers and pull-outs that show you the contents at a glance — rather than tall, deep cupboards where things hide — make daily life easier.

**Securing loose items.** Even inside a closed drawer, loose items will move during transit. Lightweight dividers, soft-walled storage cubes, or proper packing means nothing rattles. This is one of the small differences between a campervan that feels finished and one that feels like a project.

## Materials That Earn Their Cost

Storage products get hammered. Cheaper laminated MDF furniture tends to fail in predictable ways: edges chip, water swells the panels, hinges loosen as the screws back out of soft material. Two years in, the build looks tired.

Aluminium and phenolic plywood last differently. Aluminium doesn't swell or warp; phenolic plywood is engineered for exterior structural use. Heavy-duty Blum runners and hinges are over-specified for a campervan because they were built for kitchens that run for decades. None of this is glamorous — it's the bit of the build that earns its money slowly, over a lot of trips.

## Summary

A campervan storage plan that works in practice usually has the same shape:

- **Use the under-bed space properly** — pull-out drawers, not lift-up cushions
- **Add rear pull-out capacity** if you carry a lot or travel for longer trips
- **Keep overhead lockers for lightweight, frequently-used items**
- **Insist on lockable runners, positive latches, and materials that don't swell**
- **Plan for wet, dirty, and heavy gear, not just clean clothes**
